Putting It Together

A complete encounter runs patient-centred history → an early differential that decides the focused exam → focused exam → a problem list and a prioritized differential → a plan agreed with the patient → a written Problem-Oriented Clinical Record → reflection. Two rules run through it: the history drives everything (the exam and the investigations test the ideas the history raised, they do not replace them), and every problem gets its own assessment and plan, with the most likely and the most dangerous causes named first. This is the work of Consolidation of Skills, the last block of Clinical Skills in Transition to Clerkship (T2C), where you are observed doing a full history and exam and hand in written patient records. Built from the OneNote interviewing template (the history and its patient-centred questions) and the vitals final paper (how findings are written). OneNote has no material on the Consolidation weeks or the Problem-Oriented Clinical Record, so the differential section draws on upper-year notes and the record section is written to the course objectives.

Look: everything shown.

1 The general approach

  • Say it first (Consolidation): "Hello, my name is ___, I'm a medical student working with Dr. ___. I'd like to get to know you and hear about what's been going on. Is it okay if I ask you some questions today? I'll take a few notes so I can share them with the team."
  • History: let the patient tell their story first, then fill the gaps; explore what the illness means to them, not only the disease
  • Think as you go: by the end of the history of presenting illness you should have two or three working ideas; these decide which systems you examine
  • Focused exam: examine the systems that can support or rule out your working ideas, plus vital signs and general inspection every time
  • Synthesize: list the problems, then for each problem give a prioritized differential diagnosis and a plan
  • Find common ground: share your thinking in plain words and agree on the next steps with the patient
  • Write it up: the Problem-Oriented Clinical Record, organized and concise
  • Reflect: what would you do differently, and what do you need to read about?

2 The patient-centred historyT2C · Consolidation

  • Conduct a comprehensive medical, social and functional history using a patient-centred approach, addressing the relevant issues for their active problems
  • Demonstrate awareness, understanding and respect for different cultures and beliefs and how the patient sees health care, health promotion and disease management
  • Set up first: confirm identity, privacy, comfort (light, noise), and tell the patient you will take notes
  • Watch for verbal and nonverbal cues throughout: body language, speech, facial expression, affect (the outward show of emotion)
  • Signpost each change of topic: "I'd now like to move on to your past health."

Chief complaint

The patient's own reason for coming

What to do
  1. "What brings you in today?"
  2. "Is there anything else you'd like to make sure we cover?"
Common slip =Stopping at the first concern and missing the one that matters most to them

History of presenting illness (HPI)

The full story of the symptom

What to do
  1. Site, onset, character, radiation
  2. Associated symptoms, timeline, previous episodes
  3. What makes it worse or better, what they have tried, severity (1-10)
Common slip =Closed questions too early, before the patient has told the story

The illness experience (FIFE)

What the illness means to the patient

What to do
  1. Feelings: "How do you feel about these symptoms?"
  2. Ideas: "What do you think might be happening?"
  3. Function: "Has this affected work, sleep, relationships?"
  4. Expectations: "What are you hoping for today?"
Common slip =Skipping FIFE, then proposing a plan that ignores the patient's fears

Past medical history

Conditions that change the differential or the plan

What to do
  1. Major illnesses (diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, cancer)
  2. Hospital stays, surgeries, mental health history
  3. Prevention: vaccines, screening tests
Common slip =Not asking about conditions related to the chief complaint specifically

Medications and allergies

Causes and interactions

What to do
  1. Prescription, over-the-counter, herbal; dose and frequency; what each is for
  2. Allergies with the reaction (rash, swelling, breathing trouble)
Common slip =Recording "penicillin allergy" without what actually happened

Social and functional history

Who the patient is and how they live

What to do
  1. Habits, quantified: coffee, smoking or vaping, alcohol, other drugs
  2. A typical day: work, money, diet, exercise, sleep, relationships, support
  3. Safety at home
  4. Function: can they manage daily activities?
Common slip =Not quantifying (how much, how often, for how long)

Family history and review of systems

Inherited risk; symptoms the patient did not mention

What to do
  1. Parents, siblings, children: age, health, cause of death, conditions related to the complaint
  2. Review of systems: general symptoms first (weight change, fever, night sweats, fatigue), then head to toe
Common slip =A long yes/no list that crowds out the relevant systems

Closing the history

A shared understanding

What to do
  1. Summarize the key concerns back
  2. "Is there anything else that feels important for me to know?"
  3. Ask about emotional well-being
Common slip =Moving to the exam without checking the summary is right
  • Ask "why" when a habit is absent too (for example, someone who does not drink alcohol may have a past history of alcohol use disorder, or a religious reason)
  • Ask about money and access to care directly: "Do you ever have trouble making ends meet at the end of the month?" "When did you last see a doctor?"

3 From history to a focused examT2C · Consolidation

  • Construct a preliminary differential diagnosis from the history, then conduct a focused physical examination
  • Perform a focused, patient-centred physical examination based on the information gathered from the history
  • Always: vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation) and general inspection (well or unwell, distress, colour, hydration)
  • Then the systems that can confirm or rule out your working ideas; skip the ones that cannot
  • Say why you are examining each part: "Because of your cough, I'd like to listen to your lungs."

Chest pain

Heart, lungs, chest wall, upper abdomen

What to do
  1. Cardiovascular exam (pulses, jugular venous pressure, heart sounds)
  2. Respiratory exam
  3. Press on the chest wall; examine the upper abdomen
Common slip =Examining the heart only when the cause may be the lungs or the chest wall

Shortness of breath

Lungs and heart

What to do
  1. Respiratory exam (tracheal position, expansion, percussion, breath sounds)
  2. Cardiovascular exam, including leg swelling
Common slip =Missing heart failure by not checking jugular venous pressure and edema

Abdominal pain

Abdomen and nearby organs

What to do
  1. Abdominal exam (inspect, listen, tap, press; liver and spleen)
  2. Consider a pelvic, genital or rectal exam when relevant (with consent and a chaperone)
Common slip =Forgetting pelvic or testicular causes of lower abdominal pain

Back or limb pain

Joints, spine, nerves

What to do
  1. Musculoskeletal exam of the region, including the joint above and below
  2. Neurological screen: strength, sensation, reflexes; gait
Common slip =Skipping the neurological screen in back pain

Headache, weakness or numbness

Nervous system, head and neck

What to do
  1. Neurological exam (cranial nerves, motor, sensory, reflexes, coordination, gait)
  2. Head and neck exam, including the eyes
Common slip =Not checking the fundi or blood pressure in a headache
  • Write down the findings you would expect if each working idea were true before you examine, so you know what to look for

4 Building and prioritizing the differentialT2C · Consolidation

  • Formulate and prioritize a differential diagnosis, using the information gathered in the encounter, prioritizing the most urgent conditions
  • Differential diagnosis: the list of conditions that could explain a problem, in order
  • Three questions decide the order: what is most likely · what is most dangerous if missed · what is treatable and should not be delayed
  • A screening tool for causes (VITAMIN CD), from upper-year notes: Vascular · Infection or inflammation · Trauma · Autoimmune, acquired or allergic · Metabolic or mental health · Iatrogenic (caused by treatment) · Neoplasm (cancer) · Congenital or cyst · Degenerative
  • Can't-miss lists for urgent complaints: for chest pain, for example, pulmonary embolism, esophageal rupture, tension pneumothorax, myocardial infarction, aortic dissection, cardiac tamponade (upper-year mnemonic PETMAC)

Summary statement

One sentence that captures the case

What to do
  1. Age and sex, key background
  2. The main problem, how long, and its key features
  3. The most telling exam findings
Common slip =Retelling the whole history instead of choosing what matters

Generating causes

A broad enough list

What to do
  1. Think by organ (what is in the area?) and by mechanism (VITAMIN CD)
  2. Add the can't-miss diagnoses for that complaint
Common slip =Anchoring on the first idea and never widening the list

Ranking

An order you can defend

What to do
  1. Most likely first
  2. Flag the dangerous ones (⚠) even if less likely
  3. For each, give the findings for and against
Common slip =A list with no reasons, or ignoring an alarm feature

Next step

Investigations that would change the order

What to do
  1. Choose tests that separate your top diagnoses
  2. Say what result would confirm or exclude each
Common slip =Ordering every test rather than the ones that answer a question
  • Use pertinent negatives: an absent finding that makes a diagnosis less likely belongs in the reasoning ("no leg swelling, no calf tenderness")
  • If a red flag appears, say so and act: the most urgent condition is dealt with first, whatever its probability

5 The Problem-Oriented Clinical RecordT2C · Consolidation

  • Document the history, physical exam and problem list in a Problem-Oriented Clinical Record
  • Create a written record of the clinical encounter in an organized, concise manner, using the problem-oriented record as a guide
  • The record is organized by problem, not by diagnosis: each problem is listed at the level you can honestly support (a symptom, a sign, an abnormal result, or a diagnosis you are sure of)
  • Write the history and exam sections in the same order you gathered them; write findings as you observed them, including normal ones
    • for example, from the vitals final paper: "well-groomed, well-oriented, calm affect; no cyanosis, plethora, pallor, jaundice or skin signs; heart rate ___, regular, normal amplitude and contour; respiratory rate ___; blood pressure ___ in both arms; temperature ___"
  • Progress notes after the first encounter use the same problems, each written as SOAP: Subjective (what the patient reports) · Objective (exam and results) · Assessment · Plan

Identifying data and chief complaint

Who and why

What to write
  1. Age, sex, relevant background, source of history
  2. The chief complaint in the patient's words, with duration
Common slip =A diagnosis as the chief complaint ("came in with pneumonia")

History

The database

What to write
  1. HPI, including FIFE
  2. Past history, medications, allergies (with reaction)
  3. Social, functional and family history; review of systems
Common slip =Burying the HPI among the review of systems

Physical exam

What you found

What to write
  1. General inspection and vital signs
  2. Each system examined, positives and pertinent negatives
Common slip =Writing "normal" with no detail of what was checked

Problem list

Every active issue, numbered

What to write
  1. One line per problem, most important first
  2. Include psychosocial and functional problems (for example, living alone after a fall)
  3. Note inactive past problems separately
Common slip =Only medical problems, or lumping unrelated issues into one

Assessment (per problem)

Your reasoning

What to write
  1. A summary statement
  2. A prioritized differential with findings for and against
Common slip =A differential with no reasoning

Plan (per problem)

What happens next

What to write
  1. Investigations
  2. Treatment, including lifestyle: nutrition, stress management, sleep, physical activity
  3. Patient education and follow-up; who else on the team is involved
Common slip =One plan for the whole patient instead of one per problem
  • Keep it concise: a reader should find the problems and plans in seconds
  • The same structure works out loud: presenting a patient to a consultant follows the problem-oriented order (identifying data, chief complaint, key history, key findings, problem list, assessment, plan)

6 Common ground, the team and reflectionT2C · Consolidation

  • Establish common ground in the doctor-patient encounter
    • share your understanding of the problem in plain language, then ask what the patient makes of it
    • link the plan to their ideas and expectations from FIFE; agree on what happens next and what to watch for
    • if you disagree (for example, they want a test you do not think will help), explain why and find an option you can both accept
  • Based on the most likely diagnoses, research necessary investigations and develop a management plan, including evidence-based lifestyle factors such as nutrition, stress management, restorative sleep and physical activity
  • Develop an appreciation of the roles of other health care professionals in the plan (nursing, pharmacy, physiotherapy, social work, dietitian)
  • Identify personal learning issues from the encounter and build on self-reflection and faculty feedback
    • after each patient, write down one thing to read about and one thing to do differently
  • Evaluate peers' encounters and give constructive feedback
    • be specific about what you saw, name one strength and one change, and keep it about the behaviour, not the person
  • How it is assessed: at least one observed history and physical exam on a real or simulated patient, judged by a faculty facilitator against what is expected at your stage, and written patient record assessments handed in to your facilitator
