Pelvic Exam

The exam runs consent and chaperone → position and drape → look at the vulva → speculum (view the cervix, take any samples) → bimanual exam → help the patient up, then explain the findings. Two rules run through every step: say what you are about to do before you do it, and the patient can stop the exam at any time. A pregnant patient gets the obstetrical abdominal exam (Leopold's maneuvers, symphysis-fundal height, fetal heart) instead of, or before, a pelvic exam. There is no faculty material in OneNote for this session yet, and the upper-year notes do not cover the pelvic exam technique, so this note is written to the course objectives at a beginner level. The obstetrical exam steps in section 6 draw on upper-year notes.

Look: everything shown.

1 The general approach

  • Say it first (TOSCE, Teaching Objective Structured Clinical Exam): "Before we start, I'll explain each step before I do it. You can ask me to pause or stop at any point, and I will."
  • Consent: explain why the exam is needed and what it involves (a speculum to look at the cervix, then a gloved exam with two fingers), and get the patient's agreement
    • ask whether they have had a pelvic exam before, and whether anything would make it easier (some patients have had painful or traumatic experiences)
    • offer to let them have a support person present
  • Chaperone: offer a chaperone (many settings require one) and introduce them
  • Prepare: ask the patient to empty their bladder · leave the room while they undress from the waist down · gather the speculum, lubricant, gloves, light and any sample kits before they lie down
  • Explain, then touch: name each step before doing it, use neutral words ("I'm going to touch your thigh now"), and keep checking in: "How are you doing?"
  • Stop means stop: if the patient asks you to stop, stop straight away, remove the instrument gently, and talk about it

2 Positioning and drapingT2C · Gynecological Exam

  • Position and drape the patient for a pelvic exam:
    • patient lies on their back with the head slightly raised
    • heels in the footrests (stirrups), knees bent and allowed to fall apart (dorsal lithotomy position)
    • ask them to slide down until the buttocks reach the edge of the table
    • drape from the abdomen over the knees; press the drape down between the knees so you can see the patient's face and they can see you
  • Light: position the lamp before you start, so you are not adjusting it mid-exam
  • Look first: inspect the vulva (labia, clitoris, urethral opening, vaginal opening) for redness, swelling, discharge, lesions or skin changes before inserting anything

3 Speculum exam

  • Place a speculum into the vagina to view the cervix and perform any tests needed, without causing undue discomfort
  • Warm and lubricate: use lukewarm water on the speculum; if gel is needed, use a little water-based gel (more can spoil a cervical screening sample)
  • Check the speculum opens and locks before you begin; show it to the patient if they would like

Speculum insertion

Getting a view with the least discomfort

Steps
  1. Tell the patient, then touch the inner thigh first with the back of your hand
  2. Gently separate the labia
  3. Insert the closed blades slowly, angled slightly downward (toward the back), pressing on the back wall, not the front (where the urethra is)
  4. Rotate the blades level as you go in
Positive =Pain or distress: pause, check in, and stop if asked (normal: pressure, not pain)

Viewing the cervix

Cervix and its opening (os)

Steps
  1. Open the blades slowly
  2. Tilt or re-angle until the cervix sits between the blades
  3. Lock the speculum
  4. Look at colour, the os, discharge, bleeding and any growths
Positive =Lesions, ulcers, a mass, abnormal discharge or bleeding (normal: smooth pink cervix; small round os if never given birth vaginally, slit-shaped if they have)

Cervical screening sample (HPV test)

Human papillomavirus (HPV) and abnormal cervical cells

Steps
  1. Place the brush or broom in the opening of the cervix
  2. Rotate it so it samples the transformation zone (where cervical cancer starts)
  3. Rinse it into the vial as the kit directs; label it with the patient's full name, date of birth and collection date
Positive =High-risk HPV positive: the lab then checks the cells (normal: HPV negative)

Other swabs

Infection

Steps
  1. Swab the cervix for chlamydia and gonorrhea when indicated
  2. Swab the vaginal walls for discharge (yeast, bacterial vaginosis) when indicated
Positive =Positive culture or test

Speculum removal

Vaginal walls

Steps
  1. Unlock and draw back until the blades clear the cervix
  2. Let the blades close as you withdraw, never pinching the cervix or walls
  3. Look at the vaginal walls on the way out
Positive =Redness, lesions, discharge (normal: pink, moist folds)
  • Ontario screens with an HPV test from about age 25 in anyone with a cervix who has ever been sexually active; a negative test means rescreening in 5 years (PoM 2 cervical screening lecture). A sample can also be self-collected, which suits some patients better.

4 Bimanual exam

  • Perform a bimanual exam: two lubricated gloved fingers (index and middle) of one hand in the vagina, the other hand flat on the lower abdomen; you feel the organs between your two hands.
  • Tell the patient: "You'll feel my fingers now, and my other hand pressing on your tummy."
  • Structures you may feel: the cervix, the uterus, the ischial spines (to each side), the sacral promontory (at the back), and sometimes the pulse of the uterine artery. Normal ovaries usually can't be felt.

Cervix

Consistency, tenderness

Steps
  1. Find the cervix with your fingertips
  2. Feel its consistency
  3. Gently move it side to side
Positive =⚠ Cervical motion tenderness (sharp pain when the cervix moves): pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) or ectopic pregnancy (normal: firm like the tip of the nose, moves without pain)

Uterus

Size, shape, position, tenderness

Steps
  1. Fingers below the cervix, lift it upward
  2. Abdominal hand presses down just above the pubic bone
  3. Feel the uterus between your hands
Positive =Enlarged (pregnancy, fibroids) · irregular or lumpy (fibroids) · fixed or tender (normal: pear-sized, smooth, mobile, non-tender; tilted forward = anteverted, tilted back = retroverted, both normal)

Adnexa (ovaries and tubes)

Masses, tenderness

Steps
  1. Move the vaginal fingers to one side of the cervix (lateral fornix)
  2. Abdominal hand sweeps down and inward from the hip bone on that side
  3. Repeat on the other side
Positive =A mass (ovarian cyst or tumour, ectopic pregnancy) or tenderness (normal: nothing felt, or a small mobile ovary that is mildly tender)
  • Withdraw slowly and check the glove for blood or discharge.

5 Feedback during and after the exam

  • Give effective and compassionate feedback to the patient during and after the exam
  • During: say what you are doing and what you see in plain words ("Everything looks healthy so far"); praise their effort ("You're doing well; we're almost done"); watch their face and pause if they tense up
  • After:
    • tell them the exam is finished, help them slide back up and sit up, offer tissues
    • give them privacy to dress before you talk about findings, ideally face to face, sitting down
    • explain what you found, what the samples are for, when and how results come back, and what happens next
    • ask how the exam felt for them and whether they have questions

6 The obstetrical abdominal examObstetrics & Gynaecology physical exam objectives

  • Position and drape for an obstetrical exam: patient lies on their back with the head raised (or tilted slightly to the left, so the uterus does not press on the large veins), abdomen uncovered from the bottom of the ribs to the pubic bone, a drape over the pelvis and legs, and the bladder emptied
  • Each prenatal visit records weight, blood pressure, urine protein, symphysis-fundal height, presentation, fetal heart rate and fetal movement on the Ontario Perinatal Record

Symphysis-fundal height (SFH)

Fetal growth

Steps
  1. Find the top of the uterus (fundus): lay the side of your hand across the abdomen high up and walk it down until you meet the uterus
  2. Tape from the top of the pubic bone (symphysis pubis) to the fundus
  3. Record it in centimetres and plot it against gestational age
Positive =More than 2-3 cm less than the weeks of pregnancy: the baby may be growing too slowly (intrauterine growth restriction) (normal: after 20 weeks, SFH in cm ≈ weeks of pregnancy)

Leopold's 1: fundal grip

Fetal lie and presentation: what is at the top

Steps
  1. Face the patient's head
  2. Both hands feel the top of the uterus
Positive =Round, hard, movable = head (breech baby) · soft, irregular = bottom (usual, head-down baby)

Leopold's 2: lateral grip

Where the baby's back is

Steps
  1. Hands on each side of the abdomen
  2. Hold one hand steady while the other presses; then switch
Positive =Firm, smooth = back · small knobbly parts = limbs · back running up and down = longitudinal lie; across = transverse lie

Leopold's 3: pelvic grip (Pawlik's)

What is presenting above the pelvis

Steps

1. Grasp the lower abdomen just above the pubic bone between thumb and fingers of one hand

Positive =Should be the opposite of grip 1 · head that moves easily = not yet engaged in the pelvis

Leopold's 4: deep pelvic grip

Whether the head is flexed, and how far it has descended

Steps
  1. Turn to face the patient's feet
  2. Slide the fingers of both hands down the sides of the uterus toward the pubic bone
Positive =The side where the fingers meet resistance = the brow · brow opposite the back = flexed head (normal)

Fetal heart rate

Fetal well-being (discuss)

Steps
  1. Use the lie and back from Leopold's to place a handheld Doppler over the baby's back
  2. Check you are hearing the baby, not the mother's pulse (feel her wrist at the same time)
  3. Count the rate
Positive =Outside 110-160 beats per minute (normal: 110-160)
  • Feel gently, with flat hands, and stop between contractions if the patient is in labour.
