Presenting a Case

A case is presented in the same order the history was taken: identification and chief complaint → history of presenting illness (HPI) with FIFE (Feelings, Ideas, Function, Expectations) → past history, medications, allergies → habits and social history → family history → pertinent review of systems → physical exam findings → one-line summary → problem list and differential diagnosis → plan. Two rules run through it: present clearly and concisely, using the framework provided (the interviewing template), and say what matters for this problem, the findings that are present and the ones that are absent. In Year 2 the same structure becomes the written Problem-Oriented Clinical Record. Built from the Clinical Skills objectives (Introduction to Interviewing, Surgery, Consolidation), the faculty Interviewing Template, and the "Recording findings" example in the Primary Physical Exam Skills notes. The OneNote Surgery page is empty and there is no Consolidation page, so points marked (upper-year notes) come from upper-year templates, and extra marks the few points from neither.

Look: everything shown.

1 The general approach

  • Say it first (TOSCE, Teaching Objective Structured Clinical Exam): "[Name] is a [age]-year-old [occupation] who presents with [chief complaint] for [how long]."
  • Who you present to: present the case to peers and faculty (your group and facilitator) in a clear, concise manner, using the framework provided; in Surgery, to the consultant
  • Keep the order: the order of the interviewing template, every time, so the listener knows where you are
  • Tell a story: the HPI in time order, from the first symptom to today
  • Be selective: give the pertinent positives and negatives for the chief complaint; "non-contributory" covers the rest extra
  • End with your thinking: summary → what you think is going on → what you would do next
  • Then ask for feedback: reflect on the encounter and use feedback from peers, standardized patients (SPs) and facilitators to improve; give constructive feedback on your peers' encounters

2 The oral presentation, part by partFoM · Introduction to Interviewing

Identification and chief complaint

Who the patient is and why they came

What to say

"Ms. A is a 54-year-old teacher who presents with three days of chest pain."

Common slip =Leaving out how long the problem has gone on

History of presenting illness

The story of the main problem

What to say

"It began suddenly while she was walking. It is a pressure-like pain in the centre of the chest…": site, onset, character, radiation, associated symptoms, timeline, what makes it worse or better, severity

Common slip =Jumping around in time · forgetting what the patient has already tried

The patient's illness experience (FIFE)

The person, not just the disease

What to say

"She is frightened because her father had a heart attack at her age, and she hopes to find out whether this is her heart."

Common slip =Dropping FIFE from the presentation

Past medical history

Conditions that change the picture

What to say

Chronic illnesses, hospitalizations, surgeries, mental health history, vaccines and screening

Common slip =Listing every childhood illness instead of what is relevant

Medications and allergies

Safety

What to say

Each medication with dose and frequency · adherence · allergies with the reaction

Common slip =Saying "on a blood pressure pill" with no name or dose · "allergic to penicillin" with no reaction

Habits and social history

Risk factors and context

What to say

Smoking (pack-years), alcohol, drugs · work, living situation, supports · ADLs and IADLs (activities of daily living and instrumental activities of daily living) when function matters

Common slip =Skipping social history, or giving it without the impact on the illness

Family history

Inherited risk

What to say

Conditions in parents, siblings and children relevant to the complaint, with ages

Common slip ="Family history negative" without saying for what

Review of systems

Anything else found

What to say

Only the positives and the negatives that matter for this complaint

Common slip =Reading the whole review of systems aloud

Physical exam

The findings

What to say

General appearance and vital signs first (section 3), then the system examined

Common slip =Interpreting ("looks like heart failure") instead of describing what you found

3 Reporting the physical examFoM · Primary Physical Exam Skills

  • General inspection, as one sentence: "[Name] is a [age]-year-old [woman/man], well-groomed, well-oriented. Calm affect. Stable mood. No cyanosis, plethora, pallor, jaundice, or skin signs. No involuntary movements or abnormal odours."
  • Vital signs, with their quality: "Heart rate is [X], regular rhythm, normal amplitude and contour; respiratory rate is [X]; blood pressure is [X] (right and left arms); temperature is [X]."
  • When findings are abnormal, describe what you see: distress, how many words they can say per breath, position, muscle use, which arm the blood pressure was taken in
  • Then the system you examined, in its usual order (inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation, or look, feel, move)

4 Summary, differential diagnosis and planadvanced: T2C Surgery, Consolidation

  • One-line summary: age, key background, the main problem and the key findings from history and exam
  • Differential diagnosis: formulate and prioritize a differential diagnosis from the history and physical exam, most urgent conditions first
    • run the VITAMIN C&D checklist so nothing is missed: Vascular · Infection/inflammation · Trauma · Autoimmune, acquired, allergic · Metabolic, mental health · Iatrogenic (caused by treatment) · Neoplasm · Congenital, cyst · Degenerative (upper-year notes)
  • Plan: a preliminary diagnostic and therapeutic plan: what tests, what treatment, what follow-up
    • research the necessary investigations for the most likely diagnoses and develop a management plan, including evidence-based lifestyle factors: nutrition, stress management, restorative sleep and physical activity
    • appreciate the roles of other health care professionals in the patient's care
  • Common ground: establish common ground in the doctor–patient encounter: the plan you present is the one the patient agreed to, and you say what they expect

5 The write-up: Problem-Oriented Clinical Recordadvanced: T2C Consolidation

  • Document the history, physical exam and problem list in a Problem-Oriented Clinical Record, and use it to document a complete clinical encounter
  • Create the written record of the encounter in an organized, concise manner
  • To pass Consolidation: one observed history and physical exam and two written patient record assessments, submitted by your facilitator's due date

History

The full database

What to write

Every part of the interviewing template, in order, in full sentences or clear notes

Common slip =Writing it as a transcript of the conversation instead of under the template's headings

Physical exam

Findings

What to write

General appearance, vital signs, then each system examined

Common slip =Leaving out normal findings that matter for the complaint

Problem list

Every active issue

What to write

Each problem on its own line: the chief complaint first, then the others found (e.g. high blood pressure, smoking, food insecurity)

Common slip =Listing only the chief complaint

Assessment and plan, per problem

Your reasoning

What to write

For each problem: prioritized differential, then investigations, treatment and follow-up, including lifestyle advice

Common slip =One plan for everything instead of a plan for each problem extra

Learning issues

What you need to read

What to write

Personal learning issues and goals raised by this patient

Common slip =Leaving it blank

6 Presenting a surgical patientadvanced: T2C Surgery

  • Conduct a focused history and physical exam of a patient with a potential surgical condition, using surgical principles
  • Perform at least one surgical history and physical and synthesize it into a diagnosis and plan
  • Summarize one surgical history and physical for presentation to the consultant, using the Problem-Oriented Medical Record as the framework
  • Create a preliminary differential diagnosis, and a diagnostic and therapeutic plan
  • Explain how the surgical process affects the patient (patient-centred care): use FIFE: their feelings about an operation, what they think will be done, how recovery will affect work and home, and what they expect afterwards
  • For an urgent surgical problem, the history adds SAMPLE (upper-year notes):
    • Signs and symptoms · Allergies · Medications · Past medical history, including past surgeries and any reactions to anaesthesia · Last meal · Events leading up to the problem

7 After the presentationadvanced: T2C Consolidation

  • Identify personal learning issues and goals from the patient encounter
  • Build on self-reflection and faculty feedback to keep improving communication, clinical skills and clinical reasoning
