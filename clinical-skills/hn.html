Head and neck exam

The head and neck exam is done with the patient sitting on the bed or exam table, and it moves through the head in the faculty's order: eye → ear → nose → mouth → face and head → neck (lymph nodes, salivary glands, thyroid). In every region you look first, then use the instrument or your hands, and you compare left with right. Several steps are also cranial nerve tests (marked "also a cranial nerve test" below), so the same moves come back in the neurological exam. Whatever you find in the neck, describe it the same way: where it is, size, shape, mobility, tenderness and consistency. Built from the faculty's Week 7 Head & Neck objectives and anatomy pages (OneNote) and her typed notes beside them. The Ophthalmology session (section 3) has no faculty material in OneNote, so it is filled from the course syllabus objectives and upper-year notes, within them.

Look: everything shown.

1 The general approach

  • Say it first (TOSCE, Teaching Objective Structured Clinical Exam): "Hi, I'm [name], a medical student. I'd like to examine your head and neck: I'll look at your eyes, ears, nose and mouth and then feel your neck. Is that all right? Let me know if anything is uncomfortable."
  • Prepare the patient: comfort first, sitting on the bed or table, privacy (curtain drawn), warm your hands and instruments
    • clean your hands (and the stethoscope) before and after touching the patient; put on and take off personal protective equipment (PPE) properly
    • work in an orderly sequence so the patient is not moved up and down
  • General inspection: signs of distress (breathing, drooling, flared nostrils) · hair (amount, distribution, texture) · scalp (scaling, lumps) · skull (size, shape, tenderness) · face (expression, asymmetry, swelling, involuntary movements) · skin (colour, pigmentation, lesions)
  • Listen to the voice: hoarseness can mean a one-sided recurrent laryngeal nerve lesion (the nerve to the voice box)
    • ⚠ both recurrent laryngeal nerves compressed → concern for breathing difficulty (respiratory distress)
  • Then work region by region: eye → ear → nose → mouth → face → neck

2 EyePoM 1 · PPES 2 Week 7

  • Inspect (exophthalmos, ptosis, swelling, xanthelasma, jaundice and inflammation):
    • exophthalmos (proptosis): one or both eyeballs bulging forward out of the socket (for example in Graves disease)
    • eyelids: ptosis (drooping lid), swelling (edema), xanthelasma (yellow fatty plaques on the lids)
    • conjunctiva and sclera (the white of the eye): yellow = jaundice, red = inflammation
  • Know the parts: cornea, iris, pupil, lens, anterior and posterior chambers, vitreous, retina, fovea, optic nerve; from outside, eyelid, iris, sclera, pupil
  • Pupils: the light reflex has an afferent (incoming) limb, the optic nerve (cranial nerve II, CN II), and an efferent (outgoing) limb, the oculomotor nerve (CN III); either can fail on its own
  • Eye movements: six muscles move each eye (lateral, medial, superior and inferior rectus; superior and inferior oblique). CN III supplies all but two: CN IV → superior oblique, CN VI → lateral rectus. A weak muscle shows as double vision (diplopia) when looking in its direction
  • Nystagmus: rapid, involuntary to-and-fro eye movements (side to side, up and down, or circular); patients may report blurred vision, poor depth perception, imbalance, or the world seeming to move (oscillopsia)
  • Scotoma: a blind spot, an area where the patient sees partly or not at all (dark or blurry)

Inspect the eyes and eyelids

Exophthalmos, ptosis, eyelid swelling, xanthelasma, jaundice and inflammation

Steps
  1. Sit at the patient's eye level in good light
  2. Look at the eyes from the front and the side: does either bulge forward (exophthalmos)?
  3. Look at the eyelids: drooping (ptosis), swelling (edema), yellow plaques (xanthelasma)
  4. Gently pull the lower lid down; look at the conjunctiva and sclera for colour
  5. Compare left with right
Positive =Bulging eye, drooping or swollen lid, yellow plaques, yellow sclera (jaundice) or red conjunctiva (inflammation) (normal: eyes level and symmetric, white sclera, pink moist conjunctiva)

Pupils and light reflex (also a cranial nerve test: II and III)

Pupil size, regularity and reaction to light compared with the other pupil

Steps
  1. Look at the size, shape and symmetry of both pupils
  2. Patient looks into the distance
  3. Shine the light into one eye from the side
  4. Watch that pupil (direct) and the other pupil (consensual)
  5. Repeat on the other eye
  6. Near reaction: patient shifts focus to your finger brought toward the nose
Positive =Unequal, irregular, or slow or absent constriction (normal: both pupils constrict almost immediately, directly and consensually, and on near focus)

Eye movements (also a cranial nerve test: III, IV, VI)

Range of eye movement in six directions, nystagmus, and double vision in all directions

Steps
  1. Hold your finger about 45–60 cm (18–24 inches) away
  2. Patient keeps the head still and follows with the eyes only
  3. Trace an "H": far right, up, down, then far left, up, down
  4. Ask about double vision in each direction
  5. Watch for nystagmus at the end of each movement
Positive =Limited movement, misalignment, double vision in one direction, or nystagmus (normal: full, smooth movement, no diplopia)

Visual fields by confrontation (also a cranial nerve test: II)

Visual fields, tested with your index finger

Steps
  1. Sit at the patient's eye level, about an arm's length away
  2. Patient covers one eye; you close your opposite eye (mirror them)
  3. Patient looks at your nose
  4. Bring your index finger in from the edge toward the centre in each of the four quadrants, halfway between you
  5. Patient says when they first see it; repeat for the other eye
Positive =Patient sees the finger later than you do, or not at all, in a quadrant (normal: you both see it at the same time)

Visual acuity (also a cranial nerve test: II)

Visual acuity with a pocket-size chart

Steps
  1. Patient keeps on any distance glasses (note them)
  2. Hold the pocket chart at the distance printed on it
  3. Cover one eye; patient reads the smallest line they can (more than half of the letters)
  4. Repeat with the other eye
Positive =Worse than expected or different between the eyes (normal: 20/20; the top number is the test distance, the bottom the distance at which a normal eye reads that line)

Ophthalmoscope: front of the eye, then the fundus (fundus part also a cranial nerve test: II)

Iris, pupil and lens; then the optic disc (colour, sharpness, elevation), arterioles and veins, the retina between the vessels, and the macula

Steps
  1. Dim the room; set the lens dial to 0, finger on the dial to focus
  2. Patient fixes on a point in the distance
  3. From about 15° to the side of their line of sight, look at the iris, pupil and lens, and the red reflex
  4. Move in close; find a vessel and follow it to the optic disc
  5. Note the disc's colour, margins (sharpness) and any elevation, then follow the arterioles and veins outward, the retina between them, and the macula (to the temporal side of the disc)
Positive =Disc swollen with blurred margins (papilledema, raised pressure inside the skull), haemorrhages, or a missing or interrupted red reflex (normal: sharp round or oval disc, pale pink to yellow-orange, cup less than half the disc; veins larger and darker red than arteries)
  • To say: "Pupils are equal, round and reactive to light and accommodation (PERRLA)."
  • To say: "Visual acuity 20/20 in the right eye and 20/20 in the left, without glasses."

3 Full eye and vision examT2C · Ophthalmology

  • Focused eye exam with a full assessment of vision later: T2C Ophthalmology: acuity in each eye separately, visual fields, pupils with the swinging flashlight test, eye movements, and the red reflex with the ophthalmoscope
  • Ophthalmoscope: what it does and a basic look at the retina later: T2C Ophthalmology: the same steps as section 2, done on both eyes; the ophthalmoscope is a light with a dial of lenses that focuses your view from the front of the eye back to the retina
  • Slit lamp: what it does later: T2C Ophthalmology: a microscope with a thin, bright beam of light
    • shows corneal clouding (opacities) and scratches more easily than a penlight
    • with fluorescein dye and a cobalt blue light, a corneal abrasion lights up green
    • shining the beam at the edge of the cornea shows the depth of the anterior chamber (shallow in acute glaucoma)
    • shows blood (hyphema) or pus (hypopyon) in the anterior chamber

Swinging flashlight test later: T2C Ophthalmology

Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD): an optic nerve problem in one eye

Steps
  1. Dim the room; patient looks into the distance
  2. Shine the light into one eye for a few seconds
  3. Swing it quickly to the other eye
  4. Swing back and forth several times, watching the pupil you are lighting
Positive =Pupil dilates when the light swings onto it (that eye's optic nerve "sees" less light) (normal: each pupil stays constricted as the light arrives)

Red reflex later: T2C Ophthalmology

Opacity in the eye (such as cataract), a large refractive error, or misaligned eyes (strabismus)

Steps
  1. Dim the room; ophthalmoscope at 0
  2. Stand back about an arm's length
  3. Shine the light into both pupils while the patient looks straight ahead
Positive =Dull, uneven or absent red glow, or a white pupil (⚠ a white pupil in a child can mean retinoblastoma) (normal: equal bright red reflex in both eyes)

Slit lamp later: T2C Ophthalmology

Corneal clouding or scratches, depth of the anterior chamber, blood or pus in the anterior chamber

Steps
  1. Dim the room; patient seated with the chin on the rest and forehead against the bar
  2. Look through the microscope with a thin, bright beam, from the eyelid to the cornea to the anterior chamber
  3. For a scratch, add fluorescein dye and a cobalt blue light
  4. Shine the beam at the edge of the cornea to judge how deep the anterior chamber is
Positive =Cloudy cornea · green-lit abrasion · shallow anterior chamber (acute glaucoma) · blood (hyphema) or pus (hypopyon) (normal: clear cornea, no dye uptake, deep clear chamber)
  • A cataract does not cause an RAPD: light still gets around it to the optic nerve.
  • ⚠ Sudden vision loss with an RAPD and a swollen optic disc points to the optic nerve.
  • The Ophthalmology session's history objective (red eye, chronic vision loss) belongs to history-taking and is not part of this exam note.

4 EarPoM 1 · PPES 2 Week 7

  • Inspect the external ear: deformities, skin lesions, inflammation; press on the pinna and in front of the ear for tenderness or masses
    • name the parts: helix (outer rim), antihelix (inner ridge), tragus (flap in front of the canal), lobule, pinna (auricle)
  • Know the layout: external ear (pinna and ear canal) → tympanic membrane (eardrum) → middle ear (air-filled, with the ossicles malleus, incus, stapes; connected by the Eustachian tube to the back of the nose to equalize pressure) → inner ear (cochlea, semicircular canals, CN VIII)
    • the outer canal is cartilage (with hair and wax glands); the inner canal is bone, and pressing there hurts
  • Otoscope: inspect the canal and the eardrum in both ears, and note whether the canal is patent (open)
  • Hearing (CN VIII) with a 512 Hz tuning fork, whisper or finger rub

Inspect the external ear

Name the parts; deformity, skin lesions, inflammation, tenderness

Steps
  1. Look at the pinna from the side and behind: name the helix, antihelix, tragus and lobule
  2. Look for deformity, skin lesions, redness and discharge
  3. Press in front of the ear and over the mastoid behind it, and move the pinna, noting tenderness or masses
  4. Compare the two ears
Positive =Deformity, lesion, redness, swelling or tenderness (normal: symmetric, non-tender, no discharge)

Otoscopy

Ear canal (patent?) and eardrum, both ears

Steps
  1. Hold the otoscope like a pencil, your hand braced on the patient's head
  2. Pull the pinna back (up and back in an adult) to straighten the canal
  3. Insert gently into the outer (cartilage) part only
  4. Look at the canal: wax, discharge, redness, foreign body, swelling
  5. Find the eardrum landmarks: handle (manubrium), lateral process and umbo of the malleus, cone of light, pars tensa and pars flaccida
Positive =Red (inflammation; also fever or crying) · bulging (pus, acute infection) · pulled in (retracted) (Eustachian tube problem or fluid) · hole (perforation) · poor movement (fluid behind it) (normal: intact drum with the landmarks and cone of light visible, canal open)

Whisper or finger-rub test (also a cranial nerve test: VIII)

Hearing loss in one ear

Steps
  1. Patient closes their eyes; block one ear (or rub your fingers beside it to mask it)
  2. Stand so they cannot see your lips
  3. Whisper numbers, or rub your fingertips by the open ear
  4. Repeat on the other side
Positive =Cannot repeat the numbers or hear the rub → go on to Weber and Rinne (normal: hears both sides equally)

Weber test

Which ear and which kind of hearing loss

Steps
  1. Strike a 512 Hz fork
  2. Place it on the top of the head (midline)
  3. Ask: "Do you hear it in both ears, or louder on one side?"
Positive =Louder in the bad ear = conductive loss · louder in the good ear = sensorineural loss (normal: heard equally, in the middle)

Rinne test

Conductive hearing loss

Steps
  1. Place the vibrating fork on the mastoid bone behind the ear
  2. Patient says when the sound stops
  3. Move the prongs beside the ear canal straight away
  4. Ask if they can hear it again
Positive =Cannot hear it beside the ear (bone conduction louder than air) = conductive loss in that ear (normal: hears it again, air conduction louder than bone)
  • With sensorineural loss, Rinne stays normal (air louder than bone); it is Weber that points to the good ear.

5 NosePoM 1 · PPES 2 Week 7

  • Know the layout: the nasal septum separates the nostrils (nares); only the upper third of the outside of the nose is bone, the rest cartilage; three turbinates (superior, middle, inferior) stick out from each side wall
  • Inspect the outside: deviation, asymmetry, skin lesions
  • Patency of each nostril: press one nostril closed and ask the patient to breathe in through the other; repeat on the other side
  • Look inside with the otoscope and a wide speculum (or a nasal speculum and light), and avoid touching the septum
    • septal deviation: the septum bowed toward one side
    • turbinate size: the inferior and middle turbinates are the ones you see
    • mucosa normally pink, moist and smooth; look for polyps or ulcers
    • allergic rhinitis: swollen inferior turbinates, with a purplish tinge and clear discharge

Nostril patency

Whether air passes through each nostril

Steps
  1. Patient sits facing you
  2. Press one nostril closed with your finger
  3. Ask them to breathe in through the other
  4. Repeat on the other side
Positive =Little or no airflow through one nostril (swelling, a bowed septum or a polyp) (normal: air moves freely through both)

Septum and turbinates

Septal deviation and turbinate size

Steps
  1. Tilt the patient's head back a little
  2. Use the otoscope with a wide speculum (or a nasal speculum and light), lifting the tip of the nose
  3. Look at the septum, and avoid touching it
  4. Look at the inferior and middle turbinates: size and colour
  5. Check the mucosa for polyps or ulcers; repeat on the other side
Positive =Septum bowed to one side · swollen turbinates (purplish with clear discharge in allergic rhinitis) · polyps or ulcers (normal: septum central, turbinates small, mucosa pink, moist and smooth)

6 Mouth and throatPoM 1 · PPES 2 Week 7

  • Know the landmarks: the oral cavity holds the lips, the front two-thirds of the tongue, the hard palate and teeth
    • labial frenulum: the fold joining each lip to the gum · lingual frenulum: joins the underside of the tongue to the floor of the mouth
    • submandibular gland ducts open under the tongue, either side of the lingual frenulum · parotid ducts open on the inside of the cheek near the upper second molar, often marked by a small bump (papilla)
    • at the back: anterior and posterior tonsillar pillars, tonsils, soft palate, uvula
  • Inspect the lips, the tongue, the teeth and gums with a light and tongue depressor: masses, ulcers, colour change
    • tongue top and underside and the floor of the mouth (cancer risk is higher with tobacco and alcohol use)
    • throat: soft palate, uvula, pillars, tonsils: colour, symmetry, exudate, swelling, ulcers, tonsil size
  • Movements of the palate and tongue (CN IX, X, XII)

Lips, tongue, teeth, gums and throat

Masses, ulcers and colour change

Steps
  1. Look at the lips: colour, cracks, ulcers, masses
  2. Patient opens wide: look at the teeth and gums (colour, swelling, bleeding, ulcers)
  3. Look at the top of the tongue, then ask them to lift it and look at the underside and floor of the mouth
  4. Look at the inside of the cheeks, including the parotid duct opening near the upper second molar
  5. With a tongue depressor, look at the soft palate, uvula, pillars and tonsils
Positive =Mass, ulcer, white or red patch, swelling or exudate (normal: pink, moist, intact; tonsils not enlarged or coated)

Palate rise (also a cranial nerve test: IX, X)

Movement of the palate

Steps
  1. Patient opens wide (tongue depressor if needed)
  2. Ask them to say "ahh"
  3. Watch the soft palate and uvula
Positive =Palate rises on one side only, uvula pulled to one side (normal: palate rises evenly, uvula stays in the midline)

Tongue (also a cranial nerve test: XII)

Movement of the tongue

Steps
  1. Look at the tongue resting on the floor of the mouth: wasting, twitching (fasciculations)
  2. Patient sticks the tongue straight out
  3. Then moves it side to side
Positive =Tongue points to one side when stuck out (toward the weak side), wasting or fasciculations (normal: midline, moves freely)

7 Face and headPoM 1 · PPES 2 Week 7

  • Texture and distribution of hair · Skin: colour, lesions
  • Facial movements (CN VII): look at the face at rest first (symmetry, tics), then test the movements below
    • ask about taste (gustation) (CN VII carries taste from the front two-thirds of the tongue)
    • lower motor neuron lesion (for example Bell's palsy): the whole side of the face is weak, forehead included
    • upper motor neuron lesion (for example stroke): the lower face on the opposite side is weak, forehead spared
  • Strength of the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius (CN XI): look first for wasting and asymmetry of the neck and shoulders

Hair and skin

Texture and distribution of hair; skin colour and lesions

Steps
  1. Look at the hair: amount, distribution (patchy loss?) and texture (coarse, fine, brittle)
  2. Look at the scalp and the skin of the face and neck: colour, pigmentation, scaling, lumps, lesions
Positive =Patchy or diffuse hair loss, very coarse or fine hair, abnormal colour or a skin lesion (normal: even distribution, normal texture, clear skin of even colour)

Facial movements (also a cranial nerve test: VII)

Facial nerve (CN VII) weakness

Steps
  1. Wrinkle the forehead (raise the eyebrows)
  2. Close the eyes tightly; try gently to open them
  3. Bare the teeth in a broad smile
  4. Puff out the cheeks
  5. Pucker the lips
Positive =One side weaker or flatter; whole side = lower motor neuron, lower face only = upper motor neuron (normal: symmetric, strong)

Shoulder shrug and head turn (also a cranial nerve test: XI)

Trapezius and sternocleidomastoid strength

Steps
  1. Patient shrugs and holds it while you push down on both shoulders
  2. Patient turns the head to each side against your hand
Positive =Weakness or wasting on one side (normal: strong, equal)

8 Neck: lymph nodes, salivary glands, thyroidPoM 1 · PPES 2 Week 7

  • Inspect for venous distention, masses, lymph nodes, salivary gland enlargement, thyroid enlargement and masses
  • Landmarks of the neck around the thyroid (midline, top to bottom): hyoid bone (just under the jaw, moves) → thyroid cartilage with its notch ("Adam's apple") → cricoid cartilage just below → thyroid isthmus just below the cricoid, across the trachea (about the 2nd to 4th tracheal rings) → lateral lobes beside the cricoid, along the sides of the trachea
  • Name the lymph nodes of the head and neck:
    • preauricular: in front of the ear
    • posterior auricular: behind the ear, over the mastoid
    • occipital: at the back, at the base of the skull
    • tonsillar (jugulodigastric): at the angle of the jaw
    • submandibular: halfway between the angle and the tip of the jaw
    • submental: midline, just behind the tip of the jaw
    • anterior cervical: over and along the sternocleidomastoid
    • posterior cervical: along the front edge of the trapezius
    • supraclavicular: along the top of the collarbone, deep in its angle
  • Describe any node or mass by distribution, size, shape, mobility, tenderness and consistency
  • Lymphatic drainage of the thyroid: lower cervical and supraclavicular nodes, and the upper mediastinum

Neck inspection

Venous distention, masses, lymph nodes, salivary gland and thyroid enlargement

Steps
  1. Patient sits with the neck exposed and the head straight, in good light
  2. Look from the front and both sides at the neck veins, any visible mass, the lymph node areas, the parotid and submandibular glands and the thyroid area
  3. Compare left with right
Positive =Distended neck veins, visible swelling or asymmetry (normal: symmetric, no visible mass or swelling)

Lymph node palpation

Lymph nodes or masses in the neck and supraclavicular area

Steps
  1. From the front, use the pads of the index and middle fingers in small circles, light then firmer
  2. Feel both sides together to compare
  3. Go through every group in order (list above)
  4. Do the anterior cervical chain one side at a time (so you never press both carotid arteries)
Positive =Enlarged, hard, fixed or tender node (normal: none felt, or small, soft, mobile, non-tender)

Salivary glands

Parotid and submandibular enlargement and tenderness

Steps
  1. Parotid: feel in front of the ear, over the angle of the jaw
  2. Submandibular: feel under the jaw, in from its lower edge
  3. Compare the sides
Positive =Swelling or tenderness of a gland (normal: not enlarged, non-tender)

Thyroid inspection

Thyroid at rest and with swallowing

Steps
  1. Stand in front
  2. Look at the lower neck at rest
  3. Patient takes a sip of water and swallows
  4. Watch the thyroid rise with the swallow
Positive =Swelling on both sides (goitre) or one side (a thyroid mass) (normal: not visible, or a symmetric rise)

Thyroid palpation

The isthmus and the left and right lobes, from behind

Steps
  1. Stand behind the seated patient
  2. Find the thyroid cartilage, then the cricoid
  3. Just below the cricoid, feel the isthmus rise as the patient swallows a sip of water
  4. One lobe at a time: gently push the trachea toward the other side, and feel the lobe between the trachea and the sternocleidomastoid while they swallow
  5. Repeat on the other side
Positive =Enlarged, asymmetric, nodular or tender (normal: symmetrical, non-nodular, non-tender)
  • To say: "The thyroid is symmetrical, non-nodular and non-tender."
  • To say about a node: name its group and side, then its size, shape, mobility, tenderness and consistency (for example "soft, mobile and non-tender").
  • Feel one thyroid lobe at a time so you never press on both carotid arteries.
  • Thyroid bruit extra: listen over each lobe while the patient holds their breath; a bruit (a continuous whooshing sound from extra blood flow) suggests an enlarged, overactive gland such as Graves disease.
  • Look for exophthalmos at the same time: bulging eyes with an enlarged thyroid suggests Graves disease.
