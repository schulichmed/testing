Abdominal exam

The abdomen is examined in the order Inspect → Auscultate → Percuss → Palpate, then the organs (liver, spleen, aorta, kidneys) and a check for ascites (fluid in the belly). Two rules run through it: listen before you touch (pressing on the belly changes the bowel sounds), and ask where it hurts and examine that spot last, watching the patient's face the whole time.

Look: everything shown.

1 The general approach

  • Say it first (TOSCE): "Hello, I'm ___, a medical student. Can I check your name and date of birth against your wristband? I'd like to examine your tummy, which means looking, listening, tapping and pressing on it. Is that alright? Is there anywhere that hurts? I'll leave that spot until last." Then wash your hands (15 seconds), clean the stethoscope, and put on any protective equipment needed.
  • Position and draping: patient lying flat on their back (supine), relaxed and comfortable, arms at their sides or folded across the chest; uncover the abdomen and keep the rest properly draped; warm your hands and stethoscope
  • Vitals: heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, temperature, oxygen saturation (SpO2)
  • Inspect: the whole patient first (hands, face, skin), then the abdomen from the side of the bed and from the foot
  • Auscultate: bowel sounds and a bruit over the aorta, before palpation
  • Percuss: all four quadrants, then the liver and spleen
  • Palpate: light, then deep, then the organs, then ascites, then the legs for edema
    • the four quadrants are right upper (RUQ), left upper (LUQ), right lower (RLQ) and left lower (LLQ)
    • painful area last, every time

2 Before you examine: the GI historyPoM 1 · Gastroenterology

  • An organized, patient-centred history comes first later: PoM 1 Gastroenterology
    • History of the presenting illness: OPQRST (Onset, Provocation, Quality, Region/Radiation, Severity, Timing)
    • GI symptoms: nausea, vomiting (what it looks like), change in bowel movements (any blood?), diarrhea, constipation, passing gas?, abdominal pain (related to eating? to bowel movements?), fever, weight loss, blood in the stool, fatigue, rashes
    • Past history: illnesses, hospitalizations, surgeries · medications and allergies · family history
    • Social: occupation, alcohol, smoking, drugs (especially injection drug use for liver disease), diet and exercise, recent travel, sick contacts, high-risk sexual activity (liver)
    • For a liver complaint, add: dark urine, blood in the stool or vomit, itch (pruritus), easy bruising
  • Review the cardinal GI symptoms not yet covered: weight, diet, loss of appetite (anorexia), vomiting, heartburn, difficulty swallowing, excess gas, altered bowel habit, abdominal pain, bleeding, medications, itch, risk factors for liver disease
  • Then a quick review of the other systems (headache, vision, cough, palpitations, shortness of breath, chest pain, muscle aches, night sweats)

3 General inspectionFoM · PPES 1 Week 3

  • Does the patient look well or unwell? Calm or in pain? Body build and nutritional status, manner and emotional state
    • look around the bed too: stoma bag, drain, feeding tube, catheter
  • Skin and eyes: jaundice (yellow skin, yellow whites of the eyes = scleral icterus) → liver disease · pallor → anemia (GI bleeding, malabsorption)
  • Hands
    • temperature
    • clubbing → inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), celiac disease, cirrhosis
    • leukonychia (white nails) and palmar erythema (red palms) → liver disease
    • Dupuytren's contracture (a finger pulled into a bent position) → alcohol, smoking
    • thenar and hypothenar wasting (thinning of the thumb and little-finger muscles)
    • asterixis: arms out, wrists bent back; a flapping of the hands → hepatic encephalopathy (brain affected by liver failure) or kidney failure (uremia)
  • Face: central cyanosis (blue lips), parotid enlargement (swollen glands in front of the ears; can go with liver disease)
  • Chest: spider nevi (small spider-like blood vessels) and, in a male, gynecomastia (breast enlargement) → liver disease
  • Liver-focused additions later: PoM 1 Gastroenterology: Terry's nails, temporal balding
  • Mouth: lips (ulcers, cracks), tongue (cyanosis, papillae), teeth (cavities), gums (bleeding, inflammation), lining of the mouth and throat (pallor, redness, ulcers, masses) later: PoM 1 Gastroenterology
  • Tremor in the hands later: PoM 1 Gastroenterology

4 Inspect the abdomenFoM · PPES 1 Week 3

  • From the side of the bed
    • skin: scars, skin lesions, striae (stretch marks), spider nevi, hair pattern
    • dilated veins around the umbilicus (caput medusae) → high pressure in the liver's portal vein
    • bruising of the flanks (Grey Turner's sign) or around the umbilicus (Cullen's sign) → bleeding behind the abdominal lining (retroperitoneal bleed)
    • shape (contour): flat or rounded (normal), scaphoid (sunken in), or distended; asymmetry; wasting (cachexia)
    • visibly enlarged organs, masses, dilated loops of bowel
  • Movements
    • breathing: does the abdomen move normally, or is movement restricted?
    • liver and spleen descend with a deep breath
    • pulsation of the aorta (and of the liver)
    • visible peristalsis (waves of bowel movement) is abnormal, except in the very young and old (could mean a bowel obstruction)
  • Hernia check: ask the patient to lift their head off the pillow, or to cough; this raises the pressure inside the abdomen and makes a mass or an incisional hernia (a bulge through an old scar) stand out
  • From the foot of the bed: bulging flanks (fluid, ascites) and swelling of the legs

5 AuscultateFoM · PPES 1 Week 3

  • Listen before you percuss or palpate: pressing on the belly changes the bowel sounds, and it is kinder to the patient
  • Bowel sounds: listen in all four quadrants; say whether they are present or absent, and whether they are symmetric and how loud
    • normal: intermittent gurgles, roughly every 5-10 seconds
    • a constant sound, or no sound at all for more than 1 minute, is abnormal
  • Bruits (a whooshing sound from turbulent blood flow): listen over the aorta (just above the umbilicus); if you suspect arterial disease, also over the renal, iliac and femoral arteries
    • a bruit suggests a narrowed artery (stenosis) Say: "Bowel sounds present in all four quadrants; no aortic or renal bruits."

6 PercussFoM · PPES 1 Week 3

  • Percuss all four quadrants, painful area last
  • What it tells you: how much gas there is and where, whether a mass is solid or fluid-filled, and the size of the liver and spleen
  • Normal = tympany (a hollow drum note from gas); stool or normal fluid can give small patches of dullness
  • Large areas of dullness → an underlying mass or an enlarged organ (or ascites, see section 10) Say: "Tympanic to percussion in all four quadrants, no areas of dullness."

7 PalpateFoM · PPES 1 Week 3

  • Light palpation: one hand, palm and fingers together, gently, in all four quadrants, painful area last
    • feeling for discomfort (tenderness), resistance from an organ or mass, superficial masses
    • guarding (tensing the muscles): help the patient relax, then feel again; guarding that goes away is voluntary; guarding that stays is involuntary and points to irritation of the abdominal lining (peritonitis)
    • tell the examiner you are watching for involuntary guarding
  • Deep palpation: press deeply by bending the hand at the knuckles (metacarpophalangeal joints), often one hand on top of the other, in all four quadrants
    • feeling for tenderness, enlarged organs or masses
    • a mass: note its location, size, shape, consistency, tenderness and whether it pulses
  • Signs of peritonitis: rebound tenderness, pain when you percuss, guarding, pain when the bed is bumped

Head-raise test for tenderness

Whether tenderness is in the abdominal wall or inside the abdomen

Steps
  1. Find the tender spot
  2. Ask the patient to lift their head off the bed (this tenses the abdominal wall)
  3. Press on the same spot again
Positive =Pain the same or worse = the wall itself (superficial) · pain less = the tensed muscles are shielding something deeper (deep)

Aorta width

An enlarged aorta (abdominal aortic aneurysm, AAA)

Steps
  1. One hand on each side of the aorta, in the upper abdomen just above the umbilicus
  2. Press firmly and deeply
  3. Estimate the gap between your hands
Positive =Wider than 3 cm (normal: about 2 cm)

Kidneys

The lower edge (pole) of each kidney

Steps

1. Palpate bimanually (one hand behind, one hand in front) for the lower margin of each kidney

Positive =A kidney you can feel (optional in the Gastroenterology session)
  • The steps for feeling the kidneys are not in the course material beyond the objective itself: practise them in session.

8 LiverFoM · PPES 1 Week 3

  • Start low, in the right lower quadrant, so you don't miss a very large liver
  • If the edge is felt: follow the whole lower margin, feel the surface (smooth, nodular, soft, firm), then percuss the upper margin and measure the span

Liver span (percussion)

Liver size

Steps
  1. In the right midclavicular line, percuss upward from the RLQ until tympany turns dull = lower border
  2. Percuss downward from the right chest until resonance turns dull = upper border
  3. Measure the distance in centimetres
Positive =More than 12 cm = enlarged (hepatomegaly) (normal: 6-12 cm in the midclavicular line, 4-8 cm in the midsternal line)

Liver edge (palpation)

The lower margin of the liver

Steps
  1. Hand in the RLQ, just outside the right rectus muscle, fingers pointing toward the costal margin (bottom of the ribs)
  2. Patient takes deep breaths: press gently on the breath in (the liver moves down)
  3. Move the hand up on each breath out, until you meet the edge or the costal margin
  4. Or the hooking technique: hook the fingers under the rib edge, press in and up, deep breath in
Positive =An edge that touches your hand below the costal margin on a deep breath = enlarged liver (normal: usually not felt; if felt, smooth)

Murphy's sign later: PoM 1 Gastroenterology

Inflamed gallbladder (cholecystitis)

Steps
  1. Patient breathes out
  2. Hand below the right costal margin
  3. Patient breathes in
Positive =Patient winces and stops breathing in (the gallbladder hits your hand)

9 SpleenFoM · PPES 1 Week 3

  • The spleen enlarges diagonally, from the left upper quadrant toward the right lower quadrant, so you start low on the right and work up toward it

Castell's sign (percussion)

An enlarged spleen (splenomegaly)

Steps
  1. Percuss the lowest intercostal space (about the 10th) in the left anterior axillary line
  2. Percuss again with the patient in full inspiration (deep breath held), and in full expiration
Positive =Note turns dull on the deep breath in (normal: tympanic throughout)

Spleen tip (palpation)

The lower margin of the spleen

Steps
  1. Start in the RLQ, lightly, just outside the left rectus muscle as you approach
  2. Press on the breath in; move up diagonally toward the LUQ on each breath out
  3. Near the ribs, your other hand lifts the lower left rib cage
  4. Not felt? Repeat with the patient rolled onto their right side, knees bent
Positive =A spleen tip you can feel = enlarged · if felt, measure its distance below the costal margin

10 AscitesFoM · PPES 1 Week 3

  • Ascites = fluid in the peritoneal cavity (the space around the bowel). Fluid sinks; gas-filled bowel floats on top. So the flanks go dull and the top stays tympanic
  • Look first for bulging flanks (from the foot of the bed)

Shifting dullness

Ascites, by percussion

Steps
  1. Patient on their back: percuss from the midline out to the flank and mark where tympany turns dull
  2. Roll the patient onto their side and wait for the fluid to settle
  3. Percuss again and re-mark the border
Positive =The dull border moves toward the lower (dependent) side, more than 5 cm

Fluid wave

Ascites (large amounts)

Steps
  1. Patient (or a helper) presses the edge of their hand firmly down the midline (stops the wave travelling through fat)
  2. Tap one flank briskly
  3. Feel with your other hand on the opposite flank
Positive =An impulse felt on the other side, carried through the fluid

11 FinishingFoM · PPES 1 Week 3

  • With the patient still lying flat, inspect and press the legs for edema (swelling that leaves a dent = pitting edema)
  • Feel along the inguinal ligament (groin crease) for lymph nodes and hernias extra
  • Offer a digital rectal exam; don't perform one in a teaching OSCE extra
  • Thank the patient, re-cover them, wash your hands

12 Appendicitis testsadvanced: PoM 1 Gastroenterology

  • Done during palpation when the history suggests appendicitis; then offer the liver and spleen assessment

Rebound tenderness

Peritonitis (irritated abdominal lining)

Steps
  1. Press slowly and deeply
  2. Let go quickly
Positive =Pain is worse on letting go than on pressing

McBurney's point

Acute appendicitis

Steps
  1. Find the point one-third of the way from the right anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) to the umbilicus
  2. Press deeply there
Positive =Deep, localized tenderness at that point

Rovsing's sign

Appendicitis

Steps

1. Press in the left lower quadrant

Positive =Pain felt in the right lower quadrant

Psoas test

Appendicitis (the appendix lying on the psoas muscle)

Steps
  1. Patient on their back
  2. Patient lifts the right leg against your hand's resistance (contracts the psoas)
Positive =Abdominal pain (the working muscle rubs the inflamed tissue)

Obturator test

Appendicitis (the appendix lying on the obturator muscle)

Steps
  1. Patient on their back, hip and knee each bent to 90°
  2. One hand on the ankle, one on the knee
  3. Rotate the hip inward (pull the ankle out, let the knee fall in)
Positive =Abdominal pain

13 After the exam: differential and next stepsadvanced: PoM 1 Gastroenterology

  • Build and rank a differential diagnosis from the history and exam, and a first management plan
  • Abdominal pain, causes the course lists: acute appendicitis, ectopic pregnancy, ovarian cyst or torsion, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), diverticulitis, bowel obstruction, gastroenteritis
    • ⚠ in anyone who could be pregnant, think of ectopic pregnancy
  • Common first investigations
    • bloodwork: complete blood count (CBC), electrolytes, creatinine
    • liver tests: albumin, INR (clotting), bilirubin, AST, ALT, ALP (liver enzymes)
    • hepatitis serology · celiac antibody test (TTG IgA)
    • imaging: abdominal X-ray, ultrasound
